Grown-up field guide

Seven questions worth $1,000,000 each

Some math questions are so hard that nobody on Earth knows the answer yet. In 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute picked seven of them and offered $1,000,000 for each one. One has been solved so far (number 3). Chapter 8 is a bonus on Einstein's gravity. It is not a Clay problem.

How you learn here: RE, Repetitive Emulation. You meet the same big idea again and again, each time a little differently. You watch it done, copy it, then do it on your own. You keep going until it sticks.

1 HEAR ITOne big idea, said simply.
2 SEE IT 3 WAYSHands, picture, words.
3 WATCH MEFollow the steps.
4 COPY MESame steps, new numbers.
5 YOUR TURNSame move, new place.
6 DRILLGet each question right two times.
7 SAY IT BACKSay the big idea three times.

Then come back tomorrow and run it again. Orange tags mark a kid analogy, which helps you picture the idea. Blue tags mark the real math that mathematicians have to prove.

Status labels are the ones on the Clay Institute website, read on 27 Nov 2026. They can change, so check claymath.org before you repeat one.

Daily RE review

One question from every chapter, mixed up. Get each one right two times. Do it again tomorrow: repeating it on different days is what makes it stick.

Hear and see more

Math Beats Studio turns these same equations into sound and light. Try the Riemann zeros, the heat and wave equations, and the Chladni plate: mathbeatsstudio.com. Those are sonifications, which means a way to listen to numbers. They are music, not proofs.
